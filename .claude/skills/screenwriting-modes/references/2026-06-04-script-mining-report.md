## 2. Action-Line Prose Bank (the #1 deliverable)

His natural prose voice lives in these. Tagged by what each demonstrates.

1. **"MAX (31), a permanently stoned trans woman with chicken tenders in her pocket, lifts a large bag of potato skins."** (Gourmet) — character intro: two concretes + one absurd prop, prop lands last.
2. **"LISA, a woman covered in vomit stains and regret, walks over with a SCREAMING baby."** (Gourmet) — zeugma: concrete yoked to abstract in one breath. The signature intro shape.
3. **"Thursday's manager RINALDO (42), a Frenchman with a permanent stick up his ass, barges in and LAUNCHES a menu in Brandon's direction. Brandon casually dodges it."** (Gourmet) — intro + immediate physical behavior; "casually" doing the comedy.
4. **"Brandon takes a burger patty and puts it on a soggy bun with wilted lettuce, a sad tomato, and a limp pickle."** (Gourmet) — rule-of-three adjectives, each sadder; "a sad tomato" is personification at zero cost.
5. **"He vomits in the trash can nearby."** (Gourmet) — buried mid-montage between competent chef beats ("He cracks an egg into a pan with one hand while flipping pancakes with the other. / He vomits in the trash can nearby. / His hand slightly shakes as he slices strawberries.") — the rotten beat hidden in a competence list, never acknowledged.
6. **"No response. He walks over to her, CHUCKLES, and rips a fart in her face. He LAUGHS. She still doesn't respond. His smile slowly fades. She's not breathing."** (Gourmet) — the hardest pivot in the corpus. Lowest comedy to death in five sentences, no signposting. End of Act One.
7. **"Brandon walks in and sees his MOM passed out on the couch with a half-finished glass of wine, an empty wine bottle, and a cigarette cherry still lit in the ashtray. The only light in the room comes from The Food Network across the couch."** (Gourmet) — a room described as a biography. Specific objects carry the sadness; no editorializing.
8. **"A rough crowd. ROCK music blasts. The blue collar and the toothless are settled up at the bar."** (Nothing But A Good Time) — "the blue collar and the toothless": adjectives nominalized into a congregation. Pure prose voice.
9. **"Eric's a well read, decent looking guy, but compared to the other people who inhabit this bar, he's a stud."** (NBAGT) — narrator editorializes with a comparative insult to the whole room.
10. **"The walls are naked, aside from a few pictures above the desk of Hunter S. Thompson, Bukowski, Hemingway, Camus, etc."** (NBAGT) — see §10 Surprises: the gonzo lineage is original equipment.
11. **"A FASHIONABLE WOMAN, late 40's with a gross amount of work done"** / **"A WELL DRESSED MAN, early 50's with swagger"** (NBAGT) — intro economy: one detail, maximally loaded.
12. **"Doilies and Jesus figurines on every table. Multiple cats roam the living room."** (NBAGT) — set description as character study (Mrs. Thompson never needs describing after this).
13. **"Wulfgang's mansion is extravagant, covered in beautiful decor and art. An odd amount of phallic sculptures sprinkled in."** (NBAGT) — elevated register, deflating final fragment. The Hard Cut working inside an action line.
14. **"Eric stands there, in a thong, with a giant dildo in his hand. He looks at the body, then at Nadia."** (NBAGT) — the post-chaos inventory shot: freeze the absurd tableau, list its components flatly.
15. **"GROG (13), a gargantuan man-child with multiple chins and an underbite."** (LOTF) — intro: escalating physical specifics, no judgment word needed.
16. **"FLIP (9), a small girl with an oversized t-shirt and a raspy voice, lets out a WAR CRY, but then trips and falls."** (LOTF) — intro + instant deflation of the intro.
17. **"ROGUE (12) dirt-clumped hair and twitchy eyes, gnawing on the wing of a seagull, in the middle of the room."** (LOTF) — horror-specific physical detail played for comedy.
18. **"ABBY shows the island off like Vanna White."** (LOTF) — pop-culture stage direction: one reference does the whole blocking.
19. **"Zazu lays on it's back with dilated pupils."** (LOTF) — a drugged monkey conveyed in seven words, medical specificity ("dilated pupils") as the joke.
20. **"A boar SQUEALS and runs by with Flip SCREAMING on its back. / Beat. / Grog HUFFS and PUFFS as he waddles in the same direction."** (LOTF) — "Beat." as a written comedy rest; the slow follower as the second punchline.
21. **"A busy office. Close up of office supplies. Phones RING. Copies are made. Coffee drips. Most in their cubicles. Basic office hullabaloo."** (Fetish) — staccato scene-set, then a register-break colloquial summary ("Basic office hullabaloo") that tells you the narrator is a person.
22. **"SHOT OF A FUNNY THING that you would see at a park. Maybe a homeless man pooping."** (Drug Deal) — the narrator talking to himself on the page; draft-casualness as voice.
23. **"SHOT OF: Cocktail weenies. A fly SHAKES it's wings and scrapes it's legs as it crawls all over it."** (New Year's Resolutions) — opening image of the whole sketch: party promise undercut by grotesque macro detail.
24. **"Everyone LAUGHS. Lliam laughs a bit too long. / Everyone, including Father Time, stares until he stops."** (New Year's Resolutions) — social-cringe timing written as blocking.
25. **"The hotel looks like it's standing on its last leg."** + **"Allen stares at two raggedy stray dogs eating a carcass next to a food cart. A sign on the food cart reads 'Scorpion: Ten Baht'."** (Food Traveler) — place established through one terrible sign and one worse tableau.
26. **"Heather takes her finger off of her ear. In her other hand, she holds a torn-out wack-a-mole mallet. She stares at the cashier."** (A.P. Bio) — action-movie gravitas applied to a child in an arcade; the prop does it.
27. **"He's hunched over and takes a bite of his sandwich. A bunch of it falls onto his plate. A tomato falls on the ground. He looks around, picks it up, and puts it back on his sandwich."** (A.P. Bio) — pathetic-character physical comedy in flat declaratives; no adjectives, all verbs.
28. **"Gene's looking up at him with PUPPY DOG EYES. The fish has a BLANK STARE."** (Go Fish, Bob) — parallel-structure button; the second sentence is the joke because it's formatted like the first.
29. **"Gary's crying at the table drinking straight from the bottle. In one hand, a picture of his family. In the other, a picture of his briefcase and cell phone."** (I Quit) — absurd symmetry; a framed photo of a briefcase is the entire satire.
30. **"Closeup of PETEY as the spit slides down his bruised face."** (Petey Possum) — closing image as the thesis. No dialogue needed.

**Cross-cutting observations on the action-line voice:**
- **Verbs over adjectives** for physical comedy; adjectives reserved for intros, where they come in loaded triples.
- **Flat declaratives around grotesque content** — the sentence never reacts to what it reports (see §6, Under-Reaction).
- **Register breaks** ("Basic office hullabaloo," "Maybe a homeless man pooping") where the narrator's spoken voice punctures screenplay formality. This is the same instinct as his prose's colloquial closers.
- **"The kids lose their shit"** appears 3x in Petey Possum and once in New Year's ("People in Times Square lose their shit") — a real lexicon item, used as a refrain on the page.

## 3. Dialogue Joke Anatomy

**Setup length: short.** Most jokes complete in 2–4 exchange volleys. The pivot lands on the final clause of the final line before a cut.

**The post-beat addendum undercut.** A clean statement, a beat, then a qualifier that reverses it:
> RALPH: "I'm strictly selling weed now." / GERALD: "I'm happy for you, man, but I--" / RALPH: "And psychedelics too. Sometimes." (Drug Deal)

> PETE: "To remain happy and healthy... while also destroying my liver!" (New Year's)

> ERIC: "We're currently celebrating my final days as a free man... (shrugs) Or on earth." (NBAGT)

**Escalation ladder = increasing specificity, not increasing volume.** Father Time's predictions get funnier as they get more precise and dated:
> "On August 16th, Tim's podcast had the most listeners it'll ever have. Fifty-One. Oh, and that big company was Dicks Sporting Goods. Unfortunately, Dicks was shut down because people found the name 'too graphic'. Tim also gained 7 pounds." (New Year's)

This is Thompson factual-anchoring running as a pure joke engine: **exact numbers and dates ARE the punchline mechanism** (also: Heather's "There's 12 of us. If each one of us wins around 835 tickets," A.P. Bio; "Fifty dollars? For a fish?", Go Fish Bob).

**Interruption engine.** Characters constantly cut each other off mid-word ("--"); the interrupted fragment is often the joke ("You god damn bi--" hard-cutting to the baseball field, I Quit). Format-bound but maps to his prose's mid-sentence self-correction.

**Runners and callbacks, always paid off physically:**
- "you have my permission to give me a swift kick in the nads" (Gourmet cold open) → final line of pilot: **"Eddie gives Brandon a swift kick in the nads."**
- "SHOT OF: Lola looks old and decrepit." → "SHOT OF: Lola, looking decrepit." (Drug Deal, the wordless grandmother as a recurring shot)
- Wulfgang's petunias: "in return, I defecated on his..." → dying words: "if you meet Ronald... don't defecate on his petunias" (NBAGT)
- "Maybe the poison will grant you its powers!" → feast closer: "I think I even feel my hornet powers flowing through my veins." (LOTF)

**Scene buttons (the last line before a cut) are the strongest line, usually a deflation or an appalling pivot:**
> RALPH (alone, satisfied): "You lost a customer but gained a friendship... (Beat. Ralph looks over.) Hey. You, uh. You kids ever try pot?" (Drug Deal)

> Beat. / The bed collapses again. (Food Traveler — a button with no dialogue at all)

> BARTENDER: "Someone has to pay for that..." (Food Traveler — the bystander's ledger concern after the dramatic exit)

> GENE: "How was I supposed to know he wouldn't like crushed up Doritos?!" (Go Fish, Bob — grief resolved via logistics complaint)

**Sincerity gets full runway, then the world punishes it** (see §6 for promotion case). The most reliable structural joke in the corpus: an earnest speech or wholesome beat is allowed to land COMPLETELY, then violence or contempt arrives:
> PETEY: "Just as you were born to sustain life. I was born to remind people that their lives are worth living." / DOCTOR (wiping away a tear): "God damn. Who needs blood with you've got all that heart." → two scenes later, the birthday boy spits in his face. (Petey Possum)

> KYLE: "Ever since the crash... I feel like I need to protect you. We need to protect each oth--" / "Kyle JERKS his head. A dart protrudes from his neck." (LOTF — the dart arrives mid-word of the sincerity)

> Allen's redemptive apology call ("I think I understand what you were saying last night about the whole... human connection thing. I'm going t--") is interrupted by a switchblade mugging. (Food Traveler)

**In-world cheese assignment.** Hacky jokes exist in the scripts, but only in the mouths of buffoons, and someone always calls it:
> SABOTEUR: "Try and KETCHUP!" / BRANDON: "Do you guys write your own material?" (Gourmet)

> ALLEN: "Who knew Bobby Flay would be such a Bobby FLAKE. Right?!" — the character's flop-sweat is the joke; everyone at the party hates him. (Food Traveler)

This is the ancestor of the cheese-bank Satire-Redemption Rule: **cheese is always diegetic and always punished.**

## 4. Character-Intro Convention

The most concentrated voice in the corpus. The formula: **NAME (age), concrete detail + concrete detail + one zinger that lands last** — the zinger is a prop, a behavior, or an abstract noun yoked to the concrete (zeugma). Full inventory of intros with personality:

- "GERALD (20's), gaunt with an overall 'I've given up' look" (Drug Deal)
- "RALPH (30's), a large sketchy-looking man with a beanie and a hoodie. High-pitched voice." (Drug Deal)
- "BRANDON MANK (29), a chubby white guy with baggy eyes and a half-finished sleeve, works a flat top." (Gourmet)
- "EDDIE BURGOS (27), an upbeat, fast-talking Latino, and Brandon's best friend" (Gourmet)
- "NATASHA (24), a Black woman with her Thursday's suspenders dangling on each side, stares at her phone" (Gourmet)
- "MAX (31), a permanently stoned trans woman with chicken tenders in her pocket" (Gourmet)
- "RINALDO (42), a Frenchman with a permanent stick up his ass" (Gourmet)
- "LACY (5), an adorable half-Japanese girl with a short black bob still disheveled from the night before" (Gourmet)
- "RYAN, a hipster type with a beard and Oakleys on the back of his head, doing the classic 'director getting his shot' pose with his hands" (Gourmet)
- "AARON (45), a nerdy type with large glasses, a weathered chef's uniform, and loose skin under his neck, nibbles on celery" (Gourmet)
- "CARA (25), whose extremely dark circled eyes dart back and forth through her unkempt hair" (Gourmet)
- "LISA, a woman covered in vomit stains and regret" (Gourmet)

- "GROG (13), a gargantuan man-child with multiple chins and an underbite" (LOTF)
- "CHUCK (13), southern, shaved head with a few whiskers above his upper lip, wipes blood onto his apron" (LOTF)
- "VOX (16), a long-haired shirtless hippie, smokes a joint outside the hut window" (LOTF)
- "JINX (16), multi-colored hair and an outfit straight from the 80's" (LOTF)

- "JACOB, a husky youth/birthday boy" (Petey Possum) — "husky youth" is the same phrase as the reference-universe's "husky-youth era" self-description; his own childhood vocabulary applied to characters.
- "ERIC PORTER... a well read, decent looking guy, but compared to the other people who inhabit this bar, he's a stud" (NBAGT)
- "DIANE PORTER, a heavyset, raspy voiced woman in her early 50's" (NBAGT)

**Prose transfer:** this convention maps directly to how he should introduce people in essays: name them, give two physical/behavioral concretes, land the third detail as the verdict. The intro IS the joke; the rest of the piece never has to re-describe them.

## 6. New Move Candidates

Each appears in 2+ scripts unless marked provisional.

### 6.1 Comic Under-Reaction to the Appalling (STRONG — 4+ scripts)
**Mechanic:** someone says or does something horrifying; the listener responds with mild politeness, logistics, or nothing, and the scene moves on. The missing reaction IS the joke.
**Structural shape:** appalling line → flat 1-3 word response (or unrelated continuation) → scene proceeds.
> "CRAIG: --As long as it's on a corpse-- / DAN: Right. Let's just. Get back to work." (Fetish)

> "CARA: I LIKE DRUGS AND COOKING SAVED MY LIFE. / She grabs a handful of cheese and shoves it in her mouth. / BRANDON: (nodding) Nice." (Gourmet)

> "Sam looks horrified. Craig just sniffs." (Fetish — under-reaction written as blocking)

> The deli clerk watches a blood-covered man in a thong buy a six pack, "SHRUGS, and looks back at the phone... 'No judgement.'" (NBAGT)

> "WIFE: Honey... there's a young man in our front yard wearing women's underwear. He seems... frazzled. / HUSBAND: That's nice, dear. Did you take your valium?" (NBAGT)

**Prose transfer:** in essays, this is reporting the absurd flatly and letting the reader supply the scream. Already half-present in his "She cried. I nailed it." closer.

### 6.2 Sincerity Punished by the World (STRONG — 4 scripts)
**Mechanic:** an earnest beat gets FULL runway (a whole speech, a reconciliation, a redemption call), lands cleanly, and is then immediately interrupted by external violence or contempt. Distinct from self-deflection: the narrator doesn't undercut the feeling; the universe does.
> Petey's hospital monologue moves the doctor to tears → birthday boy spits in his bruised face (Petey Possum)

> "We need to protect each oth--" → dart in the neck mid-word (LOTF)

> Allen's apology call → switchblade to the cheek (Food Traveler)

> Lliam: "And mine... is to strengthen my relationships... I love you guys." → "Father Time just SIGHS." → "They broke up on January 9th." (New Year's)

**Note for skill:** this refines "gravity gets one beat before the cut." In narrative work, gravity can get a full SCENE — the deflection arrives from outside, on a delay, and hits harder for it. The current one-beat model is the compressed prose version of a bigger structural instinct.

### 6.3 The Fumbled Idiom + Bail-Out (3 scripts)
**Mechanic:** a character reaches for a stock phrase, botches or loses it, and bails with a shrug word instead of recovering. Sean's relationship to cliché: his characters can't even complete one.
> "Time to get back in there and be the hostess with the mostess... uh, host with the m-- Whatever." (Food Traveler)

> "Yea, well, you know what they say about small crews... (Allen shrugs) Big, uh... big hearts." / "JEFF (O.S): Lame!" (Food Traveler — the bail-out gets heckled)

> "That's for you to know and for me to find out. I mean... for me to know. Never mind." (Go Fish, Bob)

> "I'm interventioning. I'm. I'm intervening on you. On your drug problem." (Drug Deal — grammar fumble variant)

**Connects to:** the Cheese Gauntlet cliché-inversion instinct ("Different city. Different chapter. Same shit."). Inverting and fumbling are the two licensed ways a cliché may appear.

### 6.4 The Zeugma Paint (2 scripts + prose corpus)
**Mechanic:** yoke one abstract noun onto a list of concretes; the abstraction rides the concretes' credibility.
> "a woman covered in vomit stains and regret" (Gourmet)

> "The air reeks of burnt fat and anticipation." (Food Traveler)

> "with the rest of the yawning and the baggy-eyed" (Gourmet — adjectives nominalized into a crowd)

Prose-corpus sibling: "travel mugs filled with caffeine and hair of the dog. An elixir of motivation and a way to cope." Promote-worthy as a named micro-move.

### 6.5 The Buried Rotten Beat (2 scripts)

## 9. Screenplay→Prose Transfer Notes

**Deliberately carry into Substack prose:**
- **The character-intro formula** (§4): name + two concretes + zinger-last. Use for every person who enters an essay.
- **Comic under-reaction** (§6.1): report the absurd flatly; let the reader react.
- **The buried rotten beat** (§6.5): hide the failure mid-list, unacknowledged.
- **Scene-button discipline**: every section's last line is its strongest, usually a deflation. (Already in Success Criteria as "closers are the strongest line" — scripts show he applies it per-SCENE, not just per-piece. Section endings deserve the same pressure as essay endings.)
- **Callback payoff debt**: anything planted gets paid off, preferably physically/literally (the nads kick standard).
- **Precision-as-punchline**: exact dates, counts, and prices can BE the joke with no sensory preamble ("Fifty-One." / "835 tickets" / "Ten Baht").
- **Sincerity-punished-by-world** (§6.2): let an earnest passage land fully, then have reality (not the narrator) deliver the hit in the next paragraph.
- **"Beat." timing** → paragraph breaks and ellipses already serve this in his prose; the scripts confirm the rest-before-the-button is load-bearing, not decorative.
- **Register-break summaries** ("Basic office hullabaloo"): after formal/structured passages, one colloquial sentence that breaks the frame.

**Do NOT carry (format-bound):**
- Interruption dashes / mid-word cutoffs (needs two speakers).
- The echo sidekick ("HANK: Yea, human garbage." — needs a second voice; in prose it flattens).
- O.S./V.O./ANGLE ON machinery.
- Pun volleys — in the scripts puns ONLY exist as characterization of hacks ("Try and KETCHUP!" / "Do you guys write your own material?"). In prose, a pun in Sean's own narrator voice would be a register error. If wordplay appears, it must be slang-literalization (the 'COOK' tweet) not pun.
- Sustained grotesque/gross-out (rectal bleeding, dildo chases) — the Professional Dial caps this in prose; in scripts it's ensemble-distributed, in an essay it would all land on Sean's narrator.

## 10. Surprises (including refutations)

1. **The gonzo lineage is original equipment, not AI-era affectation.** Eric's bedroom wall in NBAGT: "a few pictures above the desk of Hunter S. Thompson, Bukowski, Hemingway, Camus, etc." — and Bukowski's "The Laughing Heart" is recited at length as the script's thesis. The skill's Thompson mode isn't a costume that was put on him during calibration; he hung it on his own wall in his 20s. **Bukowski is a missing influence in the skill's author stack** (the "finds the beauty in living in squalor" register is all over NBAGT and Gourmet, distinct from Thompson's mania).
2. **The current skill UNDER-models his sincerity capacity.** "Gravity gets one beat before the cut" is true for prose one-liners, but the scripts repeatedly give sincerity a full scene (Petey's monologue, Rogue's animated backstory, Abby and Brianna's reconciliation, every Eric/Diane scene). The deflation then comes from the WORLD, externally, on a delay. A Substack piece in his voice can sustain an earnest passage for several paragraphs IF the deflation is structural (the next thing that happens) rather than tonal (the narrator winking). The one-beat rule is the compression artifact, not the instinct.
3. **Cheese is always diegetic and always punished.** Every hacky line in the corpus is assigned to a buffoon and called out in-world (the Saboteur's puns, Allen's "Bobby FLAKE," Ben's "shit-eating grin," the Willy Wonka attribution flop). This is the cheese-bank Satire-Redemption Rule existing fully formed in ~2019-era writing — and it suggests an extension: in prose, cheese can also survive when QUOTED as someone else's speech and reacted to.
4. **The scripts are far darker than the prose corpus, and the darkness is load-bearing.** Murder played for laughs (Kyle butchered for parts: "I'll preserve some fat for Chuck to cook with!"), child stabbings, a mother dead under a fart. Peak specimen of dark-empathy: "Eh, don't worry about it. I burnt the fries too. My mom didn't even die." (Max, Gourmet) — comfort offered via competitive misfortune. The skill's gallows register is currently capped near "Luckily, their neck broke their fall"; the ceiling is higher.
5. **Biographical reality was always asserting itself.** Brandon rides the Staten Island ferry home drunk; the husky-youth junk-food era feeds two scripts' worth of Chips-Ahoy-by-the-sleeve details; the thwarted-creative-with-consultants arc is Allen's and Eric's before it was Sean's. The voice-samples finding ("biographical reality overrides prompt framing") extends backwards a decade.
6. **He drafts voice-first, format-second — archaeological proof.** A.P. Bio spec contains his raw process: blocks of run-on prose dialogue drafts left in the file below the formatted scenes ("'Hey there, Franky. I see you're right on schedule for that 12:50 dump...'"). Same finding as Round-4 calibration ("format-naïve drafting is the rule"). His drafting habit has been stable for ~7 years; build the format pass into the workflow, don't try to train it out.
7. **Two provisional moves earn promotion, one stays parked.** Faux-Ignorance Aside (4 instances, 4 scripts) and Affectionate-Insult Epithet (3+ instances, 2 scripts) clear the bar; Lyric-as-Literal clears it via the broader borrowed-line-as-speech family ("Ain't no rest for the wicked, Pop." / "L-I-V-I-N"). Rhetorical Catechism found essentially no script support — it may be a 2026-era prose tic rather than a deep pattern; keep on watch.
8. **Typos are voice data.** "balling" for bawling, "it's" for its, "your" for you're, "abord" for abort — consistent across scripts and consistent with raw interview transcripts. Relevance for `writing-humanity-pass`: copy that is TOO clean reads as un-Sean; the fix is rhythm and word choice, not introducing errors, but the pass should resist over-polishing his looser constructions ("a so called limp dick deflation" energy).
9. **One genuine anti-pattern confirmation:** nothing in 8,663 lines resembles clever-metaphor-wit about abstractions. No "void wearing a JSON costume" ancestor exists. His comedy is bodies, props, named people, food, and timing. The MEANING_OVER_ACCESS diagnosis holds against the full archaeological record.
