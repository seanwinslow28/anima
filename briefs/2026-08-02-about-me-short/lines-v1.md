## The contract (from the brief — not re-litigated here)

**The question** (Sean's only spoken line): lands right after the held quiet beat, sincere, no gag; must demonstrate method, not mere pausing (R1 failure test: "could have been asked by anyone who merely paused"); must naturally produce a SHOWN answer (the TV screen-replay — cursor circling, never finding the buy button); recruiter-legible, zero PM vocabulary, captionable, one breath.

**The button line** (the film's last audio): one line, serene, over the colorized USER's smile; the narrator never acknowledges the chaos or describes the visible joke; Goofy-grammar — inverts or double-reads the premise (the "…where you rest up for your day of rest" shape); no moral, no growth-lesson, no sincerity spike; works as a caption.

## Research notes (bounded, $0)

- **Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick): good questions ask about *behavior in the specific past*, not opinions — "talk me through the last time that happened," "how are you solving this today," "walk me through it." Opinion questions ("what's wrong?", "would you use X?") are the fail shape. The method fingerprint a PM cold-reader will recognize: **show + last time + tried**.
- **The $300M button** (Spool): the canonical story this film rhymes with — a forced-registration wall before checkout; usability *watching* (not asking opinions) found it; the fix shipped with the copy "You do not need to create an account… **Simply click Continue** to proceed to checkout." +45% purchases, +$300M/year. Resonance to exploit, never quote: watching a session recording IS the method; "simply" is both 1952-narrator diction and the actual canon fix's first word.

## Question candidates (Sean's one spoken line)

1. ⭐ **"Can you show me what happened the last time you tried to check out?"**
   Passes everything at once: *show* makes the TV replay the natural answer, *the last time you tried* is the behavior-in-the-specific-past fingerprint a working PM will clock as method, "check out" ties the human act to the CHECKOUT COMPLETIONS graph on the TV, zero jargon, one breath, captions clean. **Room recommendation.**

2. **"Can you show me your screen from when it happened?"**
   The most replay-literal cue — the answer can only arrive as a shown recording. Strain: "your screen" leans slightly technical/procedural where the beat wants warm; loses the "last time you tried" method fingerprint.

3. **"Show me what you saw."**
   Four words, the ultimate caption; humble — Sean borrowing the user's eyes instead of defending the build. Strain: reads poetic more than methodical; a cold-reading PM might credit the empathy but not the method.

4. **"Can you walk me through the last time you tried to buy something?"**
   Mom Test grammar verbatim. Strain: "walk me through" invites a *spoken* answer — the film needs a *shown* one; the replay beat has to override the verb.

5. **"Where did it lose you?"**
   Warmest of the set — the product takes the blame, which is exactly the L11 boundary ("things break and UIs get confusing"). Strain: it locates a moment without asking to observe behavior — a sympathetic anybody could ask it; borderline on the R1 failure test.

6. **"What were you trying to do?"** *(boundary specimen — included to mark the line)*
   Sincere, user-centered, and it FAILS R1: anyone who merely paused asks this. The distance between #6 and #1 is the whole point of the cold-read.

## Button-line candidates (the narrator's last audio)

1. ⭐ **"And remember: when all else fails, simply ask the user."**
   The Goofy double-read: serene manual-speak that canonizes the disaster-order we just watched as if it were the method all along — the theme lands, but only as irony, never as a moral. "Simply" is 1952-narrator diction AND the first word of the actual $300M fix copy — resonates with canon without quoting it. Nine words; caption-perfect. Strain to watch: a fast viewer could hear it as a straight moral — "when all else fails" has to carry the joke. **Room recommendation.**

2. **"And that's how to solve a problem: in five seconds, give or take a morning."**
   Precision-as-punchline (a core Sean move): the exact figure IS the joke, and "give or take a morning" serenely rounds the entire chaos away as measurement error. Fully moral-proof. Strain: it's the closest any candidate comes to acknowledging elapsed chaos — the rounding has to stay brochure-flat.

3. **"And so our team stands ready for the next problem — due any moment now."**
   The dirty-victory button: win intact, ongoing hell intact (the DAYS SINCE LAST INCIDENT: 0 energy, unspoken). Strain: gestures at the future instead of double-reading THIS film; the weakest thesis echo.

4. **"Every problem has a perfectly simple solution — once you've tried everything else."**
   Clean inversion grammar; legal per the taste rules (cliché only inverted). Strain: the Churchill-adjacent shape reads borrowed rather than Sean's.

5. **"Because the best solutions come from listening."** *(boundary specimen)*
   The moral/growth-lesson fail, included to mark the line — this is the sincerity spike the contract forbids.

## Recommended pair

**Q1 + B1.** The question demonstrates method in the exact grammar a working PM uses on the job; the button re-files that method as last-resort manual advice, which is precisely what cycle 1 dramatized. Together they bracket the film: the question closes the narrator/picture gap; the button re-opens it one inch, as a joke, on the way out.

## Cold-read (one working PM)

- Reader: Sean is grabbing a real working PM (chosen over playing the PM himself — truest to the R1 test). **Must land before boards** per the Studio Brief non-negotiable.
- Bar: "which one made them trust the asker"
- **Ready-to-run script for Sean:**
  1. Setup, verbatim: *"A checkout metric is tanking. A PM walks over to the user and gets to ask exactly one question. Here are three ways to ask it."*
  2. Read all three, unlabeled, in this order: (a) "What were you trying to do?" (b) "Can you show me what happened the last time you tried to check out?" (c) "Where did it lose you?"
  3. Ask: *"Which one makes you trust the asker most — and why?"* Capture their words.
  4. Then the button beat: describe the ending (team fixed it, user smiling, serene narrator: "Which brings us back to where every problem begins: a quiet—" ALARM, black). Ask: *"Does that ending land?"*
  5. PASS = they pick (b) or explain that (b) is the one that shows the asker knows what they're doing. If they pick (a) or (c), R1 is telling us something — bring the reaction back to the room before boards.
- **Result: PASS (2026-08-04).** Reader: anonymous, working PM. Live read, Sean relaying; PM's words captured verbatim as typed back.
  - **Question (step 3) — picked (b):** "I'm choosing B. It feels the most genuine without making the user feel stupid.  It feels more like \"Walk me through the problem\" instead of \"where did you fuck up, exactly?\""
  - **Button beat (step 4) — lands:** "Yes, that ending lands. We're right back to where we started as a PM. Solving the problem and immediately putting out the next fire. It's funny and feels like a continuous loop. Anyone who understands product and working in tech will chuckle at that ending. Even people who don't will laugh at it"
  - Against the bar ("which one made them trust the asker"): clean pass — picked (b) unprompted and reasoned from trust/genuineness.
