# How to Solve a Problem — Concept Doc

*Front-door session output, 2026-08-02/03. The About-Me centerpiece of the audited portfolio: Sean's judgement dramatized by the power of a silly cartoon, with his four AI sidekicks in full personality. Produced by the ① brainstorm front door; every fact traces to the session sidecar.*

---

## Title (options — lean first)

- **HOW TO SOLVE A PROBLEM** — my lean. It's literally the on-screen title card: the Goofy "How To" narrator frame is the piece's audio spine, so the title is already in the film. Double-reads as the recruiter's question ("can this person solve problems?") answered by watching.
- **STEP BACK** — the thesis move in two words; the beat the whole film pivots on.
- **THE FULL STACK** — the PM pun; the team is the stack.
- **PROBLEM (STILL!)** — the re-alarm gag as title; the funniest option, the least legible.

## Logline

A product manager and his four brilliant AI sidekicks answer a blaring PROBLEM! alarm with four dazzling wrong solutions — until Sean steps back, asks the user one question, and conducts the same team through a five-second fix. A portfolio self-portrait disguised as a Saturday-morning cartoon.

## Register

Pencil-test warmth wearing Tom & Jerry / Looney Tunes slapstick — Disney-pencil-test lineage with the Goofy "How To" shorts ("Two Weeks Vacation," "Father's Weekend") as the audio-mix and gag-grammar reference wells. Sincere under the silliness: the piece is about judgement, and its one quiet beat is protected. The comedy comes from the disproportion — brilliance misaimed — from Sean's own overshoot, and from the model-cliché personalities and room slapstick. The user is never the joke; the mascots are never dumb ("They're not dumb characters, they're just overthinking" — the room's rule, Sean's words). ~75–90 seconds, ceiling 2:00.

## The characters + the engine

- **Sean** — the Full Stack PM, center frame of it all. In cycle 1 he is IN the chaos — thinking he's conducting, doing a good job, egging them on. The engine is autobiographical, in his own words: *"I jump to all of the models when I'm assigned a work task and have them all build and I end up overshooting… I always need to take a step back and use their powers for good, not go crazy in all different directions."* The step-back is his actual practice, staged as a visible move: he swivels away from the monitors, breathes, and becomes the steady conductor — judgement, governance, guardrails. He speaks exactly once in the film: the question to the user.
- **Claude** (terracotta plush) — earnest, over-caveating, gentle. Wrong-build: a 40-page beautifully-structured, sticky-flagged strategy document, still growing as the alarm re-blares.
- **Codex** (terminal-faced cloud) — terse build-monotone. Wrong-build: silently rebuilds the entire checkout stack from scratch. Flawless. Ten thousand lines. Nobody asked.
- **Gemini** (star) — bubbly option-overload. Wrong-build: fifty gorgeous redesign concepts taped over every surface — new brand, new logo, seasonal themes, a shop mascot.
- **Grok** (gremlin) — feral edgelord. Wrong-build: sledgehammers the checkout wall ("what if buying was FREE?") and starts building a viral guerrilla-marketing rocket mid-room.
- **The USER** — a faceless grey pencil-test figure in a t-shirt reading "USER," small in the doorway, unnoticed. *"A user is almost always faceless unless it's a close client or customer"* — Sean's rule, and the design thesis: **the user colorizes (green) at the moment Sean understands their real problem.** Understanding is what makes users real people. Still faceless when colorful — never a specific man or woman.
- **The narrator — real Sean, self-recorded.** The Goofy-How-To serene instructional voice, walking through "steps" while the picture detonates. A callback to Sean's college thesis (a "how to" comedy video). The structural rule that runs the whole film: **the narrator/picture gap IS cycle 1 — and the gap closes at the step-back.** From the deep breath onward, narration and picture agree, and stay agreed. The form itself performs judgement arriving.

## The room

Smiling Friends break-room bones — close quarters, center-table energy traded for Sean's multi-monitor desk center-back, fridge wall, cabinets, tile floor, corner-mounted CRT — dressed as a Full Stack AI PM's headquarters: the **BACKLOG fridge** (stuffed to bursting; opening it is a hazard), the kanban corkboard with **LATER overflowing off the board and onto the floor**, the **SHIP IT brass bell wired to a confetti cannon**, the DAYS SINCE LAST INCIDENT: 0 flip-sign, a whiteboard of user-journey arrows and five scrawled WHYs, a coffee machine with five wildly different mugs. Each mascot's corner is personality in set-dressing: Claude's tidy flagged-book nook, Codex's server rack humming next to the microwave, Gemini's string-lit moodboard corner, Grok's beanbag with every dart in the wall around the dartboard and a ceiling scorch mark nobody discusses. *"It's more realistic and adds a bit of comedy… It's also closer to me working from home at my desk with my personal AI agents."*

## Structure — three movements (~75–90s)

**1 — Quiet Morning (~15s).** Title card: *"How to Solve a Problem."* The narrator opens serene over a slow pan of the room: each character doing their personality thing in their corner. The corner CRT flicks on: **PROBLEM!** over a falling CHECKOUT COMPLETIONS graph. Alarm. Nobody speaks — heads turn to Sean. He speaks; the room detonates.

**2 — The Chaos Build (~35s).** The narrator describes textbook teamwork ("Step two: gather your team's input") while the four wrong-builds escalate in rhythm, one per beat — repetition-with-variation, each more magnificent and more misaimed, Sean in the middle egging them on, conducting the storm he thinks is progress. Wrong-ship: the SHIP IT bell rings hollow, confetti fires. One beat of triumph. The alarm re-blares, louder: **PROBLEM (STILL!)**.

**3 — Step Back + Pit Stop (~30s).** Record-scratch. Sean swivels away from the monitors — eyes closed, deep breath — the film's one quiet beat, held 2–3 seconds past comfort, no gag allowed. Narration and picture agree for the first time. He walks to the doorway and asks the USER the film's only Sean-spoken line — the question. The answer plays on the big TV as a screen-recording replay: the cursor circling, circling, never finding the buy button. The room exhales — a full-cast sigh of relief. Then the **F1 pit stop** (≤5 seconds): Sean calls micro-tasks — Codex one line, Gemini make it pretty, Claude the confirmation copy, Grok demolish the old button — flawless tiny execution. The graph rockets. The USER colorizes green, beaming, still faceless. SHIP IT bell — earned this time. The narrator lands one button line over the user's smile; cut to black.

## Style + timing bible (the spine — these flow into the brief's non-negotiables)

Sourced from the room's study of "Two Weeks Vacation" (1952) and "Father's Weekend" (1953), plus the locked comedy boundary:

1. **The narrator's composure never breaks** — serene, brochure-calm, start to finish; he never acknowledges the chaos.
2. **Narrator sets up; picture punches.** No VO line ever describes a visible joke.
3. **The gap closes at the step-back** — from the deep breath onward, narration and picture agree, and stay agreed through the pit stop. The step-back beat: no gag, no VO irony, held 2–3s past comfort.
4. **Dialogue rationed:** mascots speak in short cliché bursts (the mechanic's-diagnosis register); the Sean character speaks exactly once — the question.
5. **Every beat reads on mute:** diegetic text (TV, signs, labels) carries plot; captions baked in.
6. **Squash-and-stretch and smears on every take;** face distortion is the reaction currency; move → hold → burst on the alarm and the pit stop.
7. **Repetition-with-variation:** the alarm fires twice (bigger the second); the SHIP IT bell rings twice (hollow, then earned); the wrong-builds escalate one per beat.
8. **The pit stop runs ≤5 seconds** of F1-precision choreography — precision IS the punchline — and the film ends on one narrator button line over the colorized user's smile.

## The money shot

The pit-stop-to-colorize sequence: the smallest possible fix executed with maximum team precision, the graph rocketing, and the grey USER blooming green — still faceless — as the SHIP IT bell rings for real. The whole film is built to earn that five seconds: same team, same brilliance, different aim. Judgement is the only thing that changed.

## Stress test (pre-mortem + red-team + verdict)

Run 2026-08-03: in-room reflex first, then the authority — a fresh-context reviewer reading only this doc and the Studio Brief, blind to the session. The reviewer's own words: *"This is a sound concept… I did not have to manufacture doubt."* The protected engine — the narrator/picture gap closing at the step-back — was named untouchable ("the best idea in the document"); every surviving Tiger is an execution dependency with a cheap test, not a design flaw.

**Launch-Blocking Tigers (each with its cheapest test):**

1. **The two unwritten thesis lines.** Fails if Sean's one spoken question could have been asked by anyone who merely paused — it must itself demonstrate method. Test: 5 candidate questions + 3 narrator button lines, cold-read to one working PM. *(Sean is brainstorming these in a dedicated session.)*
2. **Mute-parity of the paired audio gags.** Fails if a muted viewer can't tell hollow-ship from earned-ship, alarm-1 from alarm-2. Test: silent beat-sheet read; spec a visual twin per payoff. (Now a Studio Brief non-negotiable.)
3. **Movement 3 overload + the 60-second objective.** Fails if the step-back + question haven't landed by second 60. Test: stopwatch table-read ×2; likely fix, M3 grows to ~35–40s inside the 2:00 ceiling. (Now a Studio Brief non-negotiable.)
4. **The self-recorded narrator VO.** Fails if the read is "guy with a mic," not 1952-serene. Test: 30s scratch-record judged blind; fallback named in writing before production spend. (Now a Studio Brief non-negotiable.)
5. **The money shot is the most pipeline-hostile shot.** Pencil-test wobble flatters 85 seconds and fights the 5 where precision IS the joke. Test: a 2-second pit-stop slice as the FIRST production stub. (Now a Studio Brief non-negotiable.)

**Refuted as feared:** "the buy button trivializes PM craft" — it's the **$300M button**, canonical PM literature; a PM-hiring audience reads the trivially-simple root cause as the genre's most famous true story. **Verdict: `proceed` — Sean, 2026-08-03: "All of those make sense as next steps."**
