## The voice, in one paragraph (paste this into the ElevenLabs "style" / director notes)

A brochure. Serene, unhurried, faintly pleased with the world. Every sentence lands with a small downward
close, the way a manual ends a step. Mid-Atlantic warmth, not radio boom; think a kind museum docent who
has never once been surprised. Roughly **2.3 words a second** and never faster: the picture is doing the
running, the voice does the walking. No smile in the voice, no wink, no eyebrow. He is reading a pamphlet
he believes in. **He never acknowledges the chaos**: not a pause for it, not a lift at it, not a knowing
"hm". The straight man is the engine. The only line that changes colour is the step-back line (L9), which
is quieter and slower, agreeing with the picture for the first time. Then the brochure resumes.

## Pronunciation and emphasis

- **"Step one / two / …"** — the number carries a tiny, even stress; the colon after it is a real beat (a quarter-second), never a rush into the instruction.
- **"well-organized"** — one word, relaxed, the *or* unstressed. **"documentation"** — five even syllables, no italics on it. **"option"** — plain.
- **"solved."** — full stop energy; the pitch goes down and stays down. This is the pamphlet closing, not a cheer.
- **"Sometimes, the next step… is a step back."** — "step" is said twice and they must sound different: the first is on the way up ("the next *step*"), the second lands ("a step *back*"). The ellipsis is a genuine breath, not a dramatic pause.
- **"Which brings us back to where every problem begins: a quiet—"** — one unbroken serene arc rising gently toward the colon, a short pause, then "a quiet" *begun as if the sentence will end warmly*. Never let the voice know the alarm is coming.
- No word in the script is emphasised for irony. If a read sounds like it is in on the joke, it is the wrong read.

## Take list

Record everything at least twice (a straight take and a slightly slower one). Then these specific alternates:

**L12 — the button, three cadences, so the alarm can cut a natural word.** The written line is locked; these vary only what he was *about to* say after "quiet", which the alarm erases. The editor cuts on the alarm's transient; a few frames of the next syllable under the alarm sells the interruption.
- **Take A — dead stop.** "…where every problem begins: a quiet—" and he stops on "quiet", full serene close as if "quiet" completed the thought. The alarm hits ~120ms after the *t*. Safest for a synthetic voice; slightly less alive.
- **Take B — "…a quiet morning."** (the lean). He continues into "morning"; the alarm lands on the *m*, so the audience hears "a quiet m—". This is the natural cut: a whole next word begun, nothing of it understood. Bookends L2 ("a calm, well-organized workplace") without quoting it.
- **Take C — "…a quiet, well-organized workplace."** The full callback to Step one; the alarm cuts inside "well-". Funniest if the audience remembers the opening; riskiest, because the comma pause after "quiet" gives the alarm an obvious slot and the cut can read as designed rather than suffered. Bank it.

**L9 — the step-back line, two takes.** (i) as written, with the ellipsis breath; (ii) with no ellipsis and a slightly longer pause *before* the line instead. Keep whichever lands with the eye-open in 13.1.

**L1 — two takes:** "How to solve a problem. In eight simple steps." and, as a safety, the five words alone: "How to solve a problem." (if the card is shortened).

**L8 — two takes:** the colon version, and "And just like that. Problem solved." as two sentences (the slam may want the gap).

on-screen lettering (the hand-lettered caps of the chat box and the CRT), lower third, cream card on the paper, no drop

Diegetic text (PROBLEM!, PROBLEM (STILL!), the typed question, SHIP, BUY, USER, ANOTHER PROBLEM!) is never re-captioned:
